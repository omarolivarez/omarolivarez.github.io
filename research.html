Research

I conduct applied microeconomic research on technology and organizations, with two main areas of focus. The first examines how technologies such as artificial intelligence, robotics, and automation reshape work and organizations. The second studies the economics of organizations, focusing on coordination, organizational design, strategic human capital, and supply chains.

Working Papers and Work in Progress

Post Machina: Automation and the Organization of Work
Solo-authored
Job Market Paper
Job design has important consequences for productivity, yet little is known about how firms redesign jobs amid the growing adoption of automation. I focus on one dimension of job design, worker specialization, and examine whether firms broaden or narrow jobs when they adopt automation. I study this question using job postings from more than 190,000 U.S. firms between 2021 and 2025. I measure job breadth from the tasks described in each posting and use firms' demand for automation skills as a revealed signal of automation adoption. Using shift-share instruments that exploit differential exposure to national occupation-level automation-skill demand, I find that the effects of automation differ across automation types. Doubling robotics adoption broadens jobs by 25 percent, doubling software automation broadens them by 7 percent, and doubling AI adoption narrows them by 32 percent. These effects persist within occupations and across alternative measures of specialization. I then examine coordination as a potential mechanism. Robotics and software increase two forms of coordination: interpersonal and human-machine coordination. AI reduces both. To interpret these findings, I present a simple model in which automation changes job design by altering coordination requirements and the returns to specialization. These findings connect the task-based theories of automation to organizational design by showing that automation changes not only the allocation of tasks between workers and machines, but also the division of work among workers.
The Boundaries and Geography of Business Operations: Evidence from Product Testing
with R. Fontana, N. Greenstein, S. Greenstein, D. Kim, H. Zhang, R. Zhang
Active project
This project examines why firms shift business activities outside their organizational boundaries and even across international borders. Using newly assembled FCC certification data, we study product testing as an empirical setting in which firms, products, testing suppliers, and supplier locations are linked. Preliminary results suggest that firms organize their operations differently as their capabilities, scale, and product portfolios develop.
Balancing Low Prices and Reliable Supply in Government Pharmaceutical Procurement
Solo-authored
Active project
This project studies how reserve prices affect public pharmaceutical procurement in Brazil. It asks whether lower reserve prices reduce acquisition prices or instead discourage supplier participation, increase procurement failure and repeat purchasing, and lead to more frequent medicine stockouts in municipal health systems.

Wireless Communications Equipment Markets: Evolution, Classification, and Measurement
N. Greenstein, R. Fontana, S. Greenstein, R. Zhang, H. Zhang, O. Olivarez, D. Kim
Published in Telecommunications Policy
A central concern of many policy debates has been to foster more innovative wireless markets. How can policymakers assess whether this objective has been achieved? One measure of successful innovation is an increase in the breadth of markets addressed by new products. Existing product taxonomies, such as the North American Industry Classification System (NAICS), require new products to fit into legacy categories. Thus, they may fail to fully measure the development of new products from emerging wireless markets. This study proposes an experimental product code based on the compliance and certification applications for over 200,000 wireless devices in the Federal Communications Commission's (FCC) Equipment Authorization System (EAS) database from 1982 to 2021. The taxonomy was developed using a novel methodology, in which key phrases from application product descriptions were matched to product categories. The product code is inspired by, but diverges from, the NAICS codes. It comprises three levels of nested product aggregation: 26 Classes, 83 Families, and 298 Types, offering unique avenues to analyze the cross-market scope of wireless technology over decades. The study shows that the breadth of markets for wireless products has grown over time, particularly in areas supported by unlicensed spectrum and corresponding standards, such as Wi-Fi and Bluetooth.
